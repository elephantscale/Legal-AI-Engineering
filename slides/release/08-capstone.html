Capstone — A Private, Defensible Legal-AI Application

Elephant Scale

What You Are Building

End to end, on your own machine, with the network off if you like:

  1. Ingest a messy corpus — containers, email, scans, load files — with an exception list
  2. Search it — real eDiscovery queries, a hit report, families not orphans
  3. Answer questions over it with citations, using a local model
  4. Prove the boundary held — nothing left the building — attestation.json
  5. Measure a review against blind ground truth — recall, elusion, precision, with CIs
  6. Package it — reproducibility bundle, SAIS-100 score, container that runs offline

You are not building a demo. You are assembling the portfolio project that is the legal-engineer job description.

The capstone is integrated across Day 2 rather than bolted on at the end — students have been building it since Lab 02. This deck is the framing, the rubric, and the defense.

Say the last line plainly. Students should leave with a repository they can show a hiring manager or a client, and the six bullets above are, nearly verbatim, what a legal-tech engineering interview asks about.

The Deliverable: One Repository

my-legal-ai/
  README.md                      <- what it does, how to run it, what it claims
  docker-compose.yml             <- runs offline; internal network; pinned digests
  run_matter.py                  <- the driver: ingest -> ... -> bundle
  work/                          <- stage artifacts, checkpointed
  case-snapshot-<matter>/
    manifest.json                <- every pin
    corpus/{catalog,exceptions}.jsonl
    runs/<run-id>/{prompts/,responses.jsonl,retrieval.jsonl}
    decisions.jsonl
    validation/{protocol.md,ground_truth.jsonl,metrics.json}
    attestation.json
    VERIFY.md
  sais100-score.json             <- before and after one hardening change
  DEFENSE.md                     <- your one-page defense (next slide)
  • VERIFY.md must actually work. Someone else will run it — that is the point.

Insist on README.md and DEFENSE.md. Engineers will ship the code and skip the prose, and the prose is the differentiator in this field. A system nobody can explain is not defensible regardless of how good it is.

DEFENSE.md — Your One Page

Answer these seven, in writing, in one page. This is the document you would hand a GC.

  1. What did the system do? One paragraph a non-engineer can follow.
  2. Where did the documents go? Every hop. Cite attestation.json.
  3. How good is it? Recall, elusion, precision, with intervals. Cite metrics.json.
  4. Why was this document coded this way? Pick one real document. Show the rationale, the citations, and the rendered prompt.
  5. How do I re-run it? Point at VERIFY.md. State which bar you claim — same determinations or bit-exact.
  6. What are the limits? Where it is weak, what it cannot prove, what a human must check.
  7. Would you build this, or buy it? Which parts you would keep, replace or integrate — and what you would make the vendor prove to answer 2 and 3 for you.

Question 6 is worth more than questions 1–5 combined. A system whose author can name its limits is a system someone can rely on.

Question 7 is the one they will use first. Nobody’s first job is to rebuild Relativity; it is to decide what to buy and then make it behave. Say out loud that they cannot evaluate a product from this room and are not being asked to — what they can write, having built the thing, is the list of questions a vendor has to answer before their component stands where yours stands. Point back at Module 1’s Build vs. Buy slide: the usual answer is both, and the part that has to be yours is the part that touches the data.

Grade question 6 hardest. Students who write “no significant limitations” have failed the module, not the engineering. The honest answers in the room usually include: OCR confidence, local model quality delta, sample size, non-determinism on GPU, and the monitor’s scope.

Capstone Rubric

Dimension Wt What earns full marks
Privacy — provable 25 Local chat and embeddings and OCR; egress enforced and observed; attestation with 0 non-loopback and honest scope
Defensibility — measured 25 Blind seeded sample; pre-registered protocol; recall + elusion + precision with CIs; bundle replays via VERIFY.md
Citations — enforced 15 Verifier in code: no fabricated markers, no uncited claims, quotes verbatim; “not found” works
Ingestion — honest 15 Recursive containers; OCR; documented dedupe policy; non-empty, triaged exception list; load file read
Shippability 10 Runs offline from compose; checkpointed and resumable; pinned digests
Score & governance 10 SAIS-100 with legal overlay, before/after, no auto-fail; DEFENSE.md names real limits and ends with build/buy/integrate

Auto-fail (regardless of total): any of the four legal auto-fails from Module 7 — case content can leave by an unapproved or unenforced path; cross-matter leakage; production without recorded approval; the model cannot be pinned.

Full marks on the first two dimensions is the whole course. The other four are how you keep them.

Hand this out at the start of Day 2, not at the capstone. Students who know the rubric on Day 2 morning build toward it through every lab, which is the intended behavior.

Note the weighting: the two course claims are worth 50 together. Ingestion is 15 despite being the most work, because without the privacy and measurement layers a great pipeline is just a pipeline.

Extensions, If You Have Time

Pick one. They map to the Deep Dive and the electives.

  • Investigations — entities, a reconstructed email thread, a timeline (Module 9)
  • Agentic — a multi-step workflow with a real human checkpoint (Module 10)
  • Scale — containerize the ingest workers and scale across cores (Module 11)
  • Governance audit — a full SAIS-100 verified assessment with evidence for every sub-score (Module 12)
  • Attribution checking — use the local model as a judge: does chunk [n] actually support this sentence? Measure how often your citations are merely plausible
  • Hybrid tuning — sweep the RRF constant and top-k; show the recall curve

The attribution-checking extension is the most interesting unsolved problem in this course. If something in the room is going to turn into a product, it is that.

The attribution extension is genuinely research-adjacent and strong students love it. Be honest that LLM-as-judge attribution checking is itself imperfect, and that measuring how imperfect is the interesting part.

The Defense Round

Fifteen minutes each, in pairs. You are opposing counsel’s expert. Your job is to break your partner’s system — politely, specifically, and in writing.

Ask these, in this order:

  1. “Show me your exception list. What happened to those documents?”
  2. “What is your recall, and what is the confidence interval?”
  3. “Who chose the validation threshold, and when?”
  4. “Walk me through why document DOC-000417 was coded responsive.”
  5. “Prove no document content left this machine. What exactly does your monitor cover?”
  6. “Re-run this review in front of me.”
  7. “What model version? Can you still get those weights?”
  8. “What would you buy instead of this — and what would you make the vendor prove?”

Hand your partner their three weakest answers, in writing.

You will be asked these questions for the rest of your career in this field. Better in here first.

This exercise is the highest-value thirty minutes of the course and students consistently rate it so. Insist on the adversarial posture — politeness without pressure teaches nothing.

Question 3 catches the most people. Question 6 catches the rest. Keep the pairs mixed between software-background and legal-background students if you have both; they ask completely different questions and each finds what the other missed.

What You Walk Out With

  • The Elephant Scale Legal AI Engineer Certificate
  • The capstone repository — the evidence behind it, and the thing a hiring manager reads
  • Certification requires:
    • minimum attendance
    • completion of the module labs
    • a capstone that passes its own VERIFY.md and trips no legal auto-fail
  • What it demonstrates: you can build a legal-AI system that is private, measured, and reproducible — and you can defend it in writing

The certificate records that you cleared the bar. The repository is how you prove it, because the first thing anyone serious will ask is that you re-run your review in front of them.

Say the requirements out loud before the capstone session, not after it. The bar is objective on purpose — VERIFY.md either replays or it does not, and the four legal auto-fails either trip or they do not — which is what keeps this certificate worth having. Do not award it on effort or on attendance alone; the moment it is handed out for showing up, it is worth nothing to the person who earned it properly.

Thank You

Keep three things:

  • The four leak points — OCR, embeddings, chat, logs. Count them every time.
  • Recall and elusion — and the confidence interval. No bare point estimates.
  • “Can you prove it?” — asked of your own work, before someone else asks it of you.

Close on the three. If a student remembers nothing else in a year, those three are the ones that keep them out of trouble and make them money.

Then do the round-the-room: read back the “what I want to walk out with” answers captured on Day 1 and check them off. Cheapest and most effective close there is.
